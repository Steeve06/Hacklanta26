## Neurons: the basic unit

The nervous system is made of specialized cells called **neurons**, about 86 billion of them in the human brain. A typical neuron has three parts. **Dendrites** are branching fibers that receive signals from other neurons. The **cell body** contains the nucleus and integrates incoming signals. The **axon** is a long fiber that carries the neuron's own signal away toward other cells. Many axons are wrapped in a fatty **myelin sheath**, which insulates them and greatly speeds transmission. In multiple sclerosis the myelin is damaged, and signals slow or fail.

Neurons are supported by **glial cells**, which supply nutrients, form myelin and remove waste. Once thought of as mere glue, glia are now known to play active roles in learning and in brain health.

## The action potential

A neuron at rest has a slight negative charge inside relative to outside, maintained by the uneven distribution of charged particles, or ions, across its membrane. When incoming signals push the charge past a **threshold**, channels in the membrane open, positively charged sodium ions rush in, and an electrical impulse called an **action potential** travels down the axon.

The action potential follows the **all-or-none principle**: it either fires at full strength or not at all. A stronger stimulus does not produce a bigger impulse. Instead, it makes the neuron fire more often, and it may activate more neurons. After firing, the neuron briefly cannot fire again, a pause called the refractory period.

## Crossing the synapse

Neurons do not quite touch. They are separated by a tiny gap called the **synapse**. When an action potential reaches the end of the axon, it triggers the release of chemical messengers called **neurotransmitters** into the gap. These molecules bind to receptors on the receiving neuron, much like a key fitting a lock, and make that neuron either more or less likely to fire. Excess neurotransmitter is cleared away or reabsorbed by the sending neuron in a process called **reuptake**.

Different neurotransmitters have different roles. **Acetylcholine** is involved in muscle movement and memory. **Dopamine** is involved in movement, motivation and reward. **Serotonin** affects mood, sleep and appetite. **GABA** is the main inhibitory neurotransmitter, calming neural activity, while **glutamate** is the main excitatory one. Many medications work by altering these systems; some antidepressants, for example, block the reuptake of serotonin so that it remains active in the synapse longer.

## Organization of the nervous system

The **central nervous system** consists of the brain and spinal cord. The **peripheral nervous system** connects the central nervous system to the rest of the body. It has two parts. The **somatic** nervous system controls voluntary movements of skeletal muscles and carries sensory information. The **autonomic** nervous system controls involuntary functions such as heart rate and digestion. Its **sympathetic** branch prepares the body for action in stressful situations, the familiar fight-or-flight response, while its **parasympathetic** branch calms the body and conserves energy.

## Structures of the brain

The **brainstem**, at the base of the brain, controls basic survival functions. The **medulla** regulates breathing and heartbeat. The **cerebellum**, at the back, coordinates movement and balance and contributes to some kinds of learning. Deeper structures, often grouped as the **limbic system**, are central to emotion and memory: the **amygdala** is involved in fear and emotional responses, the **hippocampus** is critical for forming new long-term memories, and the **hypothalamus** regulates hunger, thirst, body temperature and the hormone system.

The wrinkled outer layer is the **cerebral cortex**, divided into four lobes in each hemisphere. The **frontal lobes** support planning, decision-making and voluntary movement. The **parietal lobes** process touch and spatial information. The **temporal lobes** handle hearing and aspects of language and memory. The **occipital lobes** process vision.

## Plasticity

The brain is not fixed. **Neuroplasticity** is its ability to change its structure and connections in response to experience. Learning a skill strengthens the synapses involved, and after injury, other regions can sometimes take over lost functions, especially in young people. Every time you practise something, you are physically reshaping the connections that support it.
