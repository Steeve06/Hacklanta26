## Three processes

Memory is the ability to acquire, keep and later use information. Psychologists divide it into three processes. **Encoding** is getting information into the memory system. **Storage** is keeping it over time. **Retrieval** is getting it back out when needed. A failure at any stage produces forgetting, so when you "forget" a name, it may never have been encoded properly, or it may be stored but temporarily hard to retrieve.

## A model with stages

A widely taught model, proposed by Richard Atkinson and Richard Shiffrin, describes memory as a series of stores.

**Sensory memory** holds an exact copy of incoming sensory information for a very brief time, less than a second for visual images and a few seconds for sounds. Most of it fades before we notice it. Information we attend to moves on.

**Short-term memory** holds a small amount of information for about 15 to 30 seconds unless we keep rehearsing it. George Miller famously suggested its capacity was about seven items, plus or minus two; later research suggests a smaller limit of around four meaningful units. We can stretch this capacity by **chunking**, grouping items into larger meaningful units. A phone number is easier to hold as three chunks than as ten separate digits.

Many researchers now describe short-term memory as **working memory**, an active workspace where we manipulate information, such as doing mental arithmetic or comparing two options. Working memory is limited, which is one reason multitasking harms performance on demanding tasks.

**Long-term memory** has a capacity that, for practical purposes, is unlimited, and it can hold information for a lifetime. It contains several kinds of memory. **Explicit** or declarative memories can be consciously recalled. They include **episodic** memories of personal experiences, such as your last birthday, and **semantic** memories of facts and general knowledge, such as the capital of Canada. **Implicit** or non-declarative memories influence behavior without conscious recall, such as the procedural memory for riding a bicycle or typing.

## Encoding well

How information is encoded strongly affects whether it can be retrieved. **Shallow processing**, such as noticing what a word looks like, produces weak memories. **Deep processing**, thinking about meaning and connecting new material to what you already know, produces much stronger ones. This is the basis of the **levels of processing** idea and explains why rereading notes is less effective than explaining the ideas in your own words.

Two findings about study habits are especially well supported. The **spacing effect** shows that information studied in several sessions spread over time is remembered far better than the same amount of study crammed into one session. The **testing effect** shows that practising retrieval, by quizzing yourself, strengthens memory more than additional rereading does. Short, regular quizzes on recent chapters combine both effects.

## Retrieval and its cues

Retrieval depends on cues. **Recall** requires producing information with few cues, as in a short-answer question. **Recognition** requires identifying information among options, as in multiple choice, and is generally easier. The **encoding specificity principle** states that retrieval is best when the cues present at retrieval match those present at encoding. People sometimes remember better in the same place or the same emotional state in which they learned.

## Forgetting

Hermann Ebbinghaus, testing himself on lists of nonsense syllables, found that forgetting is rapid at first and then levels off, a pattern known as the **forgetting curve**. Forgetting has several causes. Memories may fade from disuse. They may suffer **interference**: **proactive interference** occurs when old information disrupts new learning, such as writing last year's date in January, and **retroactive interference** occurs when new learning disrupts old memories.

## Memory is reconstructive

Memories are not recordings. Each time we retrieve a memory, we rebuild it from stored fragments, filling gaps with general knowledge and expectations. This makes memory efficient but also vulnerable to error. Elizabeth Loftus showed that the wording of questions can change what eyewitnesses remember: people asked how fast cars were going when they "smashed" into each other gave higher speed estimates, and were more likely to falsely remember broken glass, than people asked about cars that "hit" each other. This **misinformation effect** has serious implications for eyewitness testimony, and it is a reminder that confidence in a memory does not guarantee its accuracy.
