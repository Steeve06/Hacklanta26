## A science of mind and behavior

Psychology is the scientific study of behavior and mental processes. **Behavior** includes anything an organism does that can be observed: speaking, running, blinking, scrolling past an advertisement. **Mental processes** are internal experiences that we infer from behavior, such as thoughts, memories, emotions and perceptions. The word "scientific" is what separates psychology from everyday speculation. Psychologists make claims that can be tested, gather evidence systematically and revise their ideas when the evidence disagrees.

That last point matters because people already hold strong beliefs about behavior. We all have theories about why friends act the way they do or why we procrastinate. Many common-sense beliefs turn out to be wrong when tested. For example, people often believe that venting anger by hitting a pillow reduces aggression, yet studies have found it tends to keep angry feelings active rather than dissipate them. Psychology's value lies in checking intuitions against data.

## Roots of the field

Psychology grew out of philosophy and physiology. Philosophers had long asked how the mind relates to the body and where knowledge comes from. In the nineteenth century, physiologists began measuring reaction times and sensory thresholds, showing that mental events could be studied with experiments. In 1879 Wilhelm Wundt opened a laboratory in Leipzig devoted to psychological research, a date often used to mark the field's beginning.

Early approaches competed over what psychology should study. **Structuralists** tried to break conscious experience into basic elements through careful self-observation. **Functionalists**, influenced by Darwin, asked what mental processes are *for* and how they help organisms adapt. In the early twentieth century, **behaviorists** argued that only observable behavior could be studied scientifically and that internal states should be set aside. Behaviorism dominated American psychology for decades and produced powerful principles of learning that you will study later in this course.

## Modern perspectives

Today psychologists draw on several perspectives, each emphasizing a different level of explanation. They are best seen as complementary lenses rather than rival camps.

- The **biological** perspective looks at the brain, nervous system, hormones and genes.
- The **cognitive** perspective studies how people perceive, remember, reason and solve problems, often comparing the mind to an information-processing system.
- The **behavioral** perspective focuses on how the environment shapes behavior through learning.
- The **psychodynamic** perspective, descended from Freud, emphasizes unconscious motives and early experiences, though modern versions rely more on research than Freud did.
- The **humanistic** perspective highlights personal growth, free will and the drive toward fulfilling one's potential.
- The **sociocultural** perspective examines how other people, groups and cultural norms influence thought and behavior.
- The **evolutionary** perspective asks how behaviors might have helped our ancestors survive and reproduce.

Consider a single question: why does someone feel anxious before an exam? A biological explanation might point to stress hormones such as cortisol. A cognitive explanation might focus on thoughts like "if I fail this, I'll fail the course." A behavioral explanation might note that past exams were followed by unpleasant outcomes. A sociocultural explanation might consider family expectations or how competitive the student's environment is. Each account captures something real, and a complete understanding usually combines several.

## Many kinds of psychologists

The field is broad. **Clinical** and **counseling** psychologists assess and treat psychological difficulties. **Developmental** psychologists study change across the lifespan. **Social** psychologists study how people influence one another. **Industrial-organizational** psychologists apply research to workplaces, such as hiring and team performance. **Cognitive** psychologists and **neuroscientists** study memory, attention and the brain. Many psychologists never see clients at all; they teach, run experiments or work in industry and government.

## Thinking like a psychologist

The habits this course aims to build are curiosity, skepticism and humility. Ask what evidence supports a claim, whether there is another explanation, and how the claim could be tested. Notice when a story feels convincing simply because it matches what you already believed. These habits apply far beyond psychology: they are useful whenever someone tells you why people behave the way they do.
