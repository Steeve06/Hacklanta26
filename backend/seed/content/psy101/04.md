## From signal to meaning

**Sensation** is the process by which sense organs detect physical energy from the environment, such as light, sound waves or pressure, and convert it into neural signals. That conversion is called **transduction**. **Perception** is the process by which the brain organizes and interprets those signals into meaningful experiences: recognizing a face, hearing a word, judging that a car is approaching. Sensation gives you raw data; perception tells you what the data means.

The distinction matters because the two can come apart. Two people can receive identical sensory input and perceive different things, depending on their expectations, attention and experience.

## Thresholds

How sensitive are our senses? The **absolute threshold** is the minimum stimulation needed to detect a stimulus 50 percent of the time. Under ideal conditions, humans can see a candle flame about 50 kilometres away on a clear dark night, or hear a watch ticking about six metres away in a quiet room. Stimuli below the absolute threshold are called **subliminal**. Research suggests subliminal stimuli can produce small, brief effects in the laboratory, but claims that hidden messages in advertising can control what people buy are not supported by evidence.

The **difference threshold**, or *just noticeable difference*, is the smallest change in stimulation a person can detect half the time. **Weber's law** states that this difference is a constant proportion of the original stimulus, not a constant amount. If you are holding a 100-gram letter, you might notice an added 2 grams; holding a 1,000-gram package, you would need about 20 grams added to notice. This is why a price increase of one dollar feels large on a coffee and invisible on a car.

**Signal detection theory** adds that detection is not purely a matter of the stimulus. It also depends on the person's expectations, motivation and fatigue. A radiologist scanning for tumours or a lifeguard watching a crowded pool must balance the cost of missing a real signal against the cost of a false alarm, and their decisions shift with that balance.

## Sensory adaptation

When a stimulus is constant, our sensitivity to it decreases. This is **sensory adaptation**. You stop noticing the feel of your watch on your wrist or the hum of a refrigerator. Adaptation is useful: it frees attention for changes in the environment, which are usually more important than things that stay the same.

## Attention

At any moment far more information reaches the senses than the brain can fully process. **Selective attention** lets us focus on some of it and filter out the rest. The **cocktail party effect** describes how you can follow one conversation in a noisy room yet instantly notice when someone across the room says your name. Attention also has striking limits. In studies of **inattentional blindness**, people counting basketball passes in a video often fail to notice a person in a gorilla costume walking through the scene. Such findings are a strong argument against texting while driving.

## Bottom-up and top-down processing

**Bottom-up processing** builds perception from the sensory details upward: edges and colours combine into shapes, shapes into objects. **Top-down processing** works the other way, using knowledge and expectations to interpret the input. You can read a sentence with misspelled words because your expectations fill in what the letters should be. A **perceptual set** is a readiness to perceive something in a particular way; if you are told an ambiguous drawing shows an old woman, you are more likely to see one.

## Organizing the world

Early in the twentieth century, **Gestalt** psychologists argued that the whole we perceive is different from the sum of its parts. They described principles the brain uses to group sensations into objects:

- **Figure–ground**: we separate an object from its background.
- **Proximity**: elements close together are grouped.
- **Similarity**: elements that look alike are grouped.
- **Continuity**: we prefer smooth, continuous lines over abrupt changes.
- **Closure**: we fill in gaps to see complete shapes.

Designers use these principles constantly. Placing a label close to its input field, for instance, relies on proximity so that readers group them without effort.

## Depth and constancy

We perceive a three-dimensional world from flat images on the retina by using depth cues. **Binocular cues** rely on two eyes, such as the slight difference between the images each eye receives. **Monocular cues** work with one eye: objects that block others appear nearer, parallel lines seem to converge in the distance, and textures become finer as they recede. **Perceptual constancy** lets us see objects as having stable size, shape and colour even as the image on the retina changes, which is why a friend walking away does not seem to shrink.
