## Why we do what we do

**Motivation** refers to the processes that start, direct and sustain behavior. It explains why a student stays up finishing a project, why someone runs a marathon and why we eat when hungry. No single theory explains all motivation, so psychologists use several, each suited to different behaviors.

## Theories of motivation

Early **instinct theories** proposed that behavior is driven by inborn patterns. Instincts explain some animal behavior well, such as nest building in birds, but simply naming an instinct for every human behavior explains very little.

**Drive-reduction theory** proposes that physiological needs create unpleasant states of tension, called drives, that motivate us to satisfy the need. Hunger drives eating, which reduces the drive. The goal is **homeostasis**, a stable internal balance, much like a thermostat maintaining room temperature. Drive reduction explains basic needs but struggles to explain why people seek stimulation, such as riding roller coasters or reading thrillers.

**Arousal theory** fills that gap. It proposes that people are motivated to maintain an optimal level of arousal, neither too low nor too high. The **Yerkes–Dodson law** describes the relationship between arousal and performance: performance improves with arousal up to a point and then declines. The best level depends on the task. Easy tasks are performed best at relatively high arousal, while difficult tasks, such as a complex exam, are performed best at moderate arousal. This is one reason a little exam nervousness can help while intense anxiety hurts.

Abraham Maslow proposed a **hierarchy of needs**. Basic physiological needs, such as food and water, come first, followed by safety, then love and belonging, then esteem, and finally **self-actualization**, the drive to fulfil one's potential. Maslow argued that lower needs generally must be met before higher ones become pressing. Research has found the order more flexible than a strict hierarchy, and people sometimes pursue higher needs while basic ones are unmet, but the model remains a useful reminder that unmet basic needs can make higher goals hard to pursue.

## Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation

**Intrinsic motivation** is the desire to do something for its own sake, because it is interesting or satisfying. **Extrinsic motivation** is the desire to do something to gain a reward or avoid a penalty. Both matter, but they interact in surprising ways. In the **overjustification effect**, offering an external reward for an activity a person already enjoys can reduce their intrinsic interest. Children who enjoy drawing and are then paid to draw may draw less once the payments stop. Rewards seem to undermine intrinsic motivation most when they feel controlling, and less when they provide information about competence.

**Self-determination theory** suggests that intrinsic motivation flourishes when three needs are met: **autonomy**, feeling that one's actions are self-chosen; **competence**, feeling effective; and **relatedness**, feeling connected to others. Study environments that offer choice, achievable challenges and supportive relationships tend to sustain motivation better than pressure alone.

## The components of emotion

An **emotion** is a complex reaction involving three components: **physiological arousal**, such as a racing heart; **expressive behavior**, such as a facial expression; and **conscious experience**, including how we interpret and label the feeling. Theories of emotion disagree about how these components relate.

The **James–Lange theory** proposes that we feel emotion *because* of our bodily reactions. We see a bear, our heart races and we run, and our awareness of these reactions is the fear. In James's phrase, we feel afraid because we tremble.

The **Cannon–Bard theory** objected that very different emotions involve similar bodily arousal, and that bodily changes are often too slow to explain how quickly emotions arise. It proposes that the arousal and the conscious feeling happen simultaneously and independently.

The **two-factor theory** of Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer proposes that emotion requires both arousal and a **cognitive label**. Arousal on its own is ambiguous; we interpret it using cues from the situation. If your heart is pounding on a shaky bridge, you might attribute the arousal to fear, or, in one well-known study, to attraction toward the person you just met there.

## Expressing emotion

Some facial expressions of basic emotions, such as happiness, fear and disgust, are recognized across many cultures, suggesting a biological basis. Cultures also differ in **display rules**, the norms about when and how strongly emotions should be shown. Emotional expression is both universal and shaped by the people around us.
