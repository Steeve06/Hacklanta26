## Why methods matter

Every claim in psychology rests on how the evidence was gathered. The same question, such as whether social media affects mood, can produce very different answers depending on the method used. Knowing the strengths and limits of each method lets you judge a headline instead of simply believing it.

Research usually begins with a **theory**, a general explanation that organizes observations. From a theory, researchers derive a **hypothesis**, a specific, testable prediction. To test it they must turn vague ideas into measurable quantities through **operational definitions**. "Stress" might be operationally defined as a score on a questionnaire, a heart-rate reading or the level of cortisol in saliva. The choice affects what the study can conclude.

## Descriptive research

**Descriptive methods** aim to describe behavior as it is.

- A **case study** examines one person or group in depth. Case studies can reveal rare phenomena, such as the effects of a specific brain injury, but their results may not generalize to others.
- **Naturalistic observation** records behavior in its normal setting, such as children's play on a playground. It captures real behavior, but people may act differently when they know they are watched, and observers can see what they expect to see.
- A **survey** asks many people about their attitudes or behavior. Surveys reach large numbers cheaply, but answers depend on question wording, and people may not report honestly or accurately. A survey is only as good as its **sample**: a **random sample**, in which every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen, is the best protection against bias.

## Correlational research

**Correlational studies** measure two or more variables and examine how they relate. The relationship is summarized by a **correlation coefficient**, written *r*, which ranges from −1 to +1. A positive correlation means the variables rise together; a negative correlation means one rises as the other falls. Values near zero indicate little linear relationship.

Correlations are useful for prediction, but they cannot by themselves show cause and effect. Suppose hours of sleep correlate with exam scores. Perhaps more sleep improves performance. Perhaps students who are already doing well feel less pressure to stay up late. Or perhaps a **third variable**, such as a part-time job, reduces both sleep and study time. The phrase to remember is that **correlation does not imply causation**.

## Experiments

The **experiment** is the method that can establish cause and effect. The researcher manipulates one variable, the **independent variable**, and measures its effect on another, the **dependent variable**. Participants exposed to the manipulation form the **experimental group**; those who are not form the **control group**.

The crucial step is **random assignment**: each participant has an equal chance of being placed in either group. Random assignment spreads pre-existing differences, such as motivation or ability, evenly across groups on average. If the groups then differ on the dependent variable, the manipulation is the most likely cause. Note the difference between random *sampling*, which concerns who is in the study, and random *assignment*, which concerns which condition they experience.

Experiments must also guard against expectations. In a **single-blind** study, participants do not know which condition they are in. In a **double-blind** study, neither participants nor the researchers interacting with them know, which prevents researchers from unintentionally influencing results. A **placebo**, an inactive treatment that looks like the real one, lets researchers separate the effect of a treatment from the effect of simply believing you received it.

## Evaluating findings

**Statistical significance** indicates that a result would be unlikely if there were truly no effect, but it says nothing about whether the effect is large or important. A finding becomes more trustworthy when other researchers **replicate** it. In the 2010s, attempts to repeat well-known studies found that a substantial share did not replicate, prompting reforms such as registering hypotheses in advance and sharing data openly.

## Ethics

Research with people must protect them. Institutional review boards evaluate proposed studies. Participants give **informed consent**, may withdraw at any time without penalty, have their data kept confidential and, if any deception was used, receive a **debriefing** that explains the true purpose of the study afterwards.
