## People in context

**Social psychology** studies how people think about, influence and relate to one another. Its central lesson is that situations shape behavior more powerfully than we tend to assume. Ordinary people can act in surprising ways when the social context changes.

## Explaining behavior

When we see someone act, we automatically form explanations, called **attributions**. A **dispositional attribution** explains behavior by the person's traits: she was late because she is careless. A **situational attribution** explains it by circumstances: she was late because the bus broke down.

People systematically overestimate the role of personality and underestimate the role of the situation when explaining *other people's* behavior. This tendency is called the **fundamental attribution error**. Interestingly, we reverse the pattern for ourselves: when *we* are late, we notice the traffic. The error is stronger in individualistic cultures, which emphasize personal traits, than in collectivistic cultures, which pay more attention to context and relationships.

## Attitudes and dissonance

An **attitude** is an evaluation of a person, object or idea. We usually assume attitudes drive behavior, but behavior can also change attitudes. **Cognitive dissonance theory**, proposed by Leon Festinger, holds that we feel uncomfortable tension when our actions conflict with our beliefs, and we often reduce that discomfort by changing our beliefs to match what we did. In a classic study, participants paid only one dollar to tell another person that a boring task was interesting later rated the task as more enjoyable than participants paid twenty dollars. The well-paid group could justify the lie by the money; the poorly paid group resolved the tension by deciding the task had not been so bad.

## Conformity

**Conformity** is adjusting one's behavior or thinking to match a group. In Solomon Asch's experiments in the 1950s, participants judged which of three lines matched a reference line, an easy task. When several confederates first gave an obviously wrong answer, participants went along with the group on about a third of the trials, and most participants conformed at least once.

Two pressures explain conformity. **Normative influence** is the desire to be liked and to avoid rejection. **Informational influence** is the belief that the group may know something we do not. Conformity increases when the group is unanimous and when responses are public, and it drops sharply if even one other person dissents.

## Obedience

Stanley Milgram studied **obedience** to authority. Participants were told they were taking part in a study of learning and were instructed by an experimenter to give increasingly strong electric shocks to a "learner" each time he made a mistake. In reality, the learner was an actor and received no shocks. Despite the learner's protests and screams, about two-thirds of participants in the original version continued to the maximum level when the experimenter calmly insisted.

Obedience was highest when the experimenter was physically present and seen as legitimate, and when the victim was distant. It dropped when participants saw others refuse. Milgram's studies raised serious ethical concerns about the stress placed on participants and contributed to modern research protections. They also offered a sobering insight: harmful acts are not committed only by unusual people; they can emerge from ordinary people responding to powerful situational pressures.

## Helping and the bystander effect

In 1964 news reports claimed that many neighbours witnessed an attack on a woman named Kitty Genovese without helping. Later investigation showed the reports were exaggerated, but the case inspired research by John Darley and Bibb Latané on when people help. They found the **bystander effect**: a person is less likely to help in an emergency when other people are present. One reason is **diffusion of responsibility**, the sense that someone else will act. Another is that people look to others to decide whether a situation is really an emergency, and if everyone looks calm, each concludes that nothing is wrong.

Knowing about the bystander effect can reduce it. If you need help in a crowd, point to one specific person and ask them directly. If you witness an emergency, assume that no one else has acted yet.

## Groups

Groups also change how individuals perform and decide. The presence of others tends to improve performance on easy, well-practised tasks and impair it on difficult ones, a pattern called **social facilitation**. In groups, people sometimes exert less effort when individual contributions are not identifiable, known as **social loafing**. And tight-knit groups that value agreement can fall into **groupthink**, suppressing doubts to maintain harmony and making poor decisions as a result. Assigning someone to argue the other side and inviting outside opinions are simple safeguards.
