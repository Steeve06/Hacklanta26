## What counts as learning

Psychologists define **learning** as a relatively lasting change in behavior or knowledge that results from experience. The definition excludes changes caused by maturation, fatigue or drugs. Learning lets organisms adapt to their environments during their own lifetimes rather than relying only on inherited reflexes. Three kinds of learning have been studied most closely: classical conditioning, operant conditioning and observational learning.

## Classical conditioning

In the early 1900s the Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov was studying digestion in dogs when he noticed that the dogs began to salivate before food arrived, at the sight of the assistant who fed them. He set out to study this systematically.

Pavlov's procedure introduced terms still in use today. Food in the mouth is an **unconditioned stimulus (US)**: it naturally triggers salivation, an **unconditioned response (UR)**, without any learning. A tone is initially a **neutral stimulus**: it does not cause salivation. Pavlov repeatedly sounded the tone just before presenting food. After several pairings, the tone alone produced salivation. The tone had become a **conditioned stimulus (CS)**, and salivation to the tone was now a **conditioned response (CR)**.

Several processes shape conditioning. **Acquisition** is the initial learning of the association; it works best when the CS comes shortly before the US. **Extinction** occurs when the CS is repeatedly presented without the US, and the CR weakens. Yet after a pause, the CR may briefly return, a phenomenon called **spontaneous recovery**, showing that extinction suppresses rather than erases the learning. **Generalization** is the tendency to respond to stimuli similar to the CS, while **discrimination** is learning to respond only to the specific CS.

Classical conditioning helps explain emotional reactions. In a famous and ethically troubling 1920 study, John Watson and Rosalie Rayner conditioned a baby known as Little Albert to fear a white rat by pairing it with a loud noise. Today, therapies for phobias use the same principles in reverse, gradually exposing people to feared situations without harm so that the fear response extinguishes.

## Operant conditioning

Classical conditioning involves automatic responses. **Operant conditioning** involves voluntary behavior and its consequences. B. F. Skinner studied it with animals in a chamber where pressing a lever could produce food. The core idea, built on Edward Thorndike's **law of effect**, is that behaviors followed by favourable consequences become more likely, and behaviors followed by unfavourable consequences become less likely.

**Reinforcement** increases a behavior. **Positive reinforcement** adds something pleasant, such as praise after finishing homework. **Negative reinforcement** removes something unpleasant, such as a car's seatbelt alarm stopping once you buckle up. Note that "negative" means *removing* a stimulus, not that the outcome is bad.

**Punishment** decreases a behavior. **Positive punishment** adds something unpleasant, such as a speeding fine. **Negative punishment** removes something pleasant, such as losing phone privileges. Punishment can suppress behavior, but it tells the learner only what not to do, may produce fear or resentment, and often works only while the punisher is present. Reinforcing a desired alternative behavior is usually more effective.

Complex behaviors can be taught through **shaping**, reinforcing successive approximations of the target behavior. Animal trainers and teachers both use shaping.

## Schedules of reinforcement

How often reinforcement follows a behavior matters. **Continuous reinforcement**, rewarding every response, produces fast learning but fast extinction once rewards stop. **Partial reinforcement** produces slower learning but much more persistent behavior. Partial schedules come in four types:

- **Fixed-ratio**: reward after a set number of responses, such as a free coffee after ten purchases.
- **Variable-ratio**: reward after an unpredictable number of responses, as with slot machines. This schedule produces high, steady response rates that are very resistant to extinction.
- **Fixed-interval**: reward for the first response after a set time, which tends to produce a burst of activity just before the reward is due, like studying the night before a scheduled exam.
- **Variable-interval**: reward for the first response after unpredictable time periods, like checking for messages.

## Observational learning

People also learn by watching others. In Albert Bandura's **Bobo doll** studies, children who watched an adult hit an inflatable doll were more likely to imitate that aggression when given the chance. Observational learning requires attention, memory of what was seen, the ability to reproduce the behavior and the motivation to do so. It explains how we pick up skills, habits and social norms without directly experiencing their consequences.
