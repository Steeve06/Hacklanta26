## Why it matters

Surveys of employers consistently rank communication among the skills they value most in new hires, often above technical knowledge. The reason is practical. Almost every task in an organization depends on someone understanding someone else: a manager explaining priorities, a team agreeing on a deadline, a company answering a customer's complaint. Poor communication costs time, money and trust. A vague email produces three follow-up questions; an unclear specification produces work that must be redone.

Business communication differs from other kinds of writing in its purpose. It exists to get something done. Its readers are busy, they read selectively, and they want to know quickly what is being asked of them and why. Good business writing respects that.

## A model of communication

Communication can be described as a process with several parts.

- The **sender** has an idea to share.
- The sender **encodes** the idea into a **message** using words, images, tone or gestures.
- The message travels through a **channel**, such as email, a meeting, a phone call or a report.
- The **receiver** **decodes** the message, interpreting what it means.
- The receiver provides **feedback**, a response that shows how the message was understood.

The model highlights that meaning is not simply transferred. The receiver constructs meaning from the message using their own knowledge, expectations and mood. Two readers can decode the same email very differently. Feedback is how the sender discovers whether understanding matches intention, which is why one-way communication is riskier than conversation.

## Noise

Anything that interferes with accurate communication is called **noise**. Some noise is physical, such as a poor video connection or a loud office. Much of it is not.

- **Semantic noise** arises when words mean different things to different people. Jargon, acronyms and vague terms such as "soon" or "a few" are common culprits.
- **Psychological noise** comes from the receiver's state of mind: stress, distraction, or assumptions about the sender.
- **Cultural noise** arises when norms differ, for example about how direct a request should be or how quickly a reply is expected.
- **Information overload** occurs when the receiver gets more messages than they can process, so important ones are skimmed or missed.

Reducing noise starts with anticipating it. Define terms that might be unfamiliar. Replace "soon" with a date. Put the most important point first, so that a reader who stops early still gets it. Choose a channel suited to the message.

## Choosing a channel

Channels differ in **richness**, how many cues they carry and how quickly feedback can flow. Face-to-face conversation is the richest: it carries tone, facial expression and immediate questions. Video and phone calls are slightly less rich. Instant messages and email are leaner, and formal reports leaner still.

Rich channels suit messages that are complex, sensitive or likely to be misunderstood, such as delivering difficult news or resolving a disagreement. Lean channels suit routine, factual messages and anything that needs a permanent record, such as confirming the details of an agreement. A common mistake is using a lean channel for an emotional message, such as criticizing a colleague's work by email, where the absence of tone invites the worst possible reading.

## Know your audience

Before drafting anything substantial, effective communicators analyze their **audience**. Useful questions include:

- Who will read this, and is there a secondary audience, such as a manager who receives it forwarded?
- What do they already know about the topic?
- What do they care about, and how will this affect them?
- How are they likely to react: receptive, neutral or resistant?
- What do I want them to do after reading?

The answers shape every later choice. A technical update for engineers can use specialized terms that would confuse a client. A request to a resistant reader needs more explanation of benefits than a request to an eager one. Writing that skips audience analysis tends to be organized around what the writer wants to say rather than what the reader needs to know.

## Ethics and credibility

Finally, communication carries ethical weight. Misleading by omission, burying unfavourable information or overstating results may achieve a short-term goal but damages credibility, which is slow to rebuild. Professionals who communicate honestly and clearly, even when the news is unwelcome, become the people others trust with important work.
