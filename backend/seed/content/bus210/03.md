## The workhorse channel

Email remains the main written channel in most organizations, even as chat tools and collaboration platforms have grown. Office workers commonly receive dozens to more than a hundred messages a day. In that environment, the writer's job is to make each message easy to understand, easy to act on and easy to find later.

## Subject lines

The subject line decides whether and when a message is opened. A good subject line is **specific** and, where possible, states the action needed. Compare:

- "Question" versus "Approve venue deposit by Thursday?"
- "Update" versus "Q3 survey results: response rate up to 41%"

Some teams use short prefixes such as "Action:", "FYI:" or "Decision needed:" to help readers sort their inbox. Update the subject line when a thread's topic changes, so the message can be found later.

## Openings and structure

Most work emails should follow the direct approach. Put the request or main point in the **first sentence or two**, then provide the details. A reader should be able to understand what you need without scrolling.

Weak opening: "I hope you're well. I wanted to reach out regarding the event we discussed a couple of weeks ago, as there have been some developments."

Strong opening: "Can you approve the $1,200 deposit for the Riverside venue by Thursday? The venue will release our date to another group on Friday."

After the opening, give the reader what they need to act: relevant facts, options, attachments. Use short paragraphs and, when there are several items, a numbered list so that the reader can respond point by point. If you need several separate decisions, consider whether they belong in separate messages.

Close with a clear **next step**: what you need, from whom, by when. "Please reply by Thursday noon so I can confirm with the venue" is more useful than "Let me know your thoughts."

## Professional norms

A few habits mark polished email.

- **Use a greeting and a closing** that match the relationship. "Hi Priya," is fine for most colleagues; a first message to an external client may warrant something more formal.
- **Reply All carefully.** Before replying to everyone, ask whether every recipient needs your response.
- **Use CC and BCC deliberately.** CC signals that someone should be informed; it is not a way to apply pressure. BCC hides recipients and can feel deceptive if discovered; it is most appropriate for large mailings where recipients should not see each other's addresses.
- **Attach what you mention,** and mention what you attach. Name attachments clearly.
- **Proofread names and numbers** before sending. A misspelled name or wrong date undermines credibility quickly.
- **Assume permanence.** Email can be forwarded, archived and retrieved in legal proceedings. Do not write anything you would be uncomfortable seeing shared widely.

## Instant messaging and team chat

Chat tools such as Slack or Microsoft Teams suit quick questions, coordination and informal updates. They have their own norms.

- Write a complete message rather than sending "hi" and waiting for a reply. "Hi Marcus, do you have the final version of the pricing sheet? I need it for the 3 pm client call" lets the reader respond in one step.
- Use **threads** to keep conversations organized.
- Respect **availability**. Status indicators and time zones exist for a reason; mark non-urgent messages as such outside working hours.
- Move to a call when a thread goes back and forth more than a few times without resolution. Long chat debates are a sign that a richer channel would be faster.

## When not to write

Some messages should not be sent digitally at all. Delivering serious bad news, giving critical feedback on personal performance, or addressing conflict usually goes better in a conversation, ideally in person or on video, where tone and immediate questions can be handled. A written follow-up can then confirm what was agreed.

Similarly, avoid sending messages while angry. A useful rule is to draft the message, leave the recipient field empty so it cannot be sent accidentally, and reread it after a break.

## Managing your own inbox

Clear writing helps your readers; good habits help you. Many professionals check email at set times rather than continuously, use folders or labels sparingly, and follow a simple rule for each message: act on it, schedule it, delegate it or archive it. The goal is to respond reliably without letting email consume the day.
