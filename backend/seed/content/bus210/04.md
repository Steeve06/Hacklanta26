## Goals of a bad-news message

Every organization must sometimes say no: rejecting a job applicant, declining a customer's request, announcing a price increase or reporting a delayed project. A bad-news message has several goals at once. It must deliver the news clearly, so there is no confusion. It must help the reader accept it, by explaining the reasons. It must preserve goodwill, so the relationship survives. And it must protect the organization, by avoiding statements that create legal liability or false promises.

Balancing these goals is hard. Too blunt, and the reader feels dismissed. Too soft, and the reader may not realize the answer is no.

## The indirect plan

For many bad-news messages, especially to customers, clients or people outside the organization, writers use an **indirect plan** with four parts.

**1. Buffer.** Open with a neutral or positive statement related to the topic. The buffer shows that you are considering the reader and prepares them for the reasons. Effective buffers might express appreciation ("Thank you for your detailed proposal for the spring campaign"), state agreement on something ("We share your goal of reducing delivery times") or provide relevant good news. A buffer should be **relevant** and **sincere**. It must not suggest a yes is coming, because the reversal will feel like a trick.

**2. Reasons.** Explain the reasons for the decision before stating it. Clear, logical reasons help the reader see the decision as fair rather than arbitrary. Focus on reasons the reader can understand and, where possible, on how the decision benefits them or others: "To keep prices stable for all members, we limit refunds to the first 30 days." Avoid hiding behind vague phrases such as "company policy" without explanation.

**3. The bad news.** State the decision clearly but without dwelling on it. Several techniques help. Place the news in the middle of a paragraph rather than at the start or end. Use the passive voice to depersonalize it: "The application deadline has passed" rather than "You missed the deadline." Emphasize what you *can* do: "Although the order cannot ship this week, we can deliver it by the 14th." Never leave the reader unsure of the answer; implication is acceptable only if the message is unmistakable.

**4. Goodwill close.** End on a forward-looking, positive note. Offer an alternative, suggest a next step, or express appreciation. The close should be specific to the situation. Avoid clichés such as "If you have any questions, please do not hesitate to contact us," and never apologize again at the end, which pulls attention back to the bad news.

## An example

A customer asked for a refund on a software subscription after the 30-day refund period.

> Thank you for using Planwise for your team's scheduling over the past two months, and for telling us what didn't work for you. Your feedback about recurring events has gone straight to our product team.
>
> Our subscription refunds are available during the first 30 days, which lets us keep monthly prices low for every customer. Because your subscription began on March 2, it is outside that period and isn't eligible for a refund.
>
> We have, however, cancelled automatic renewal, so you won't be charged again, and your account will stay active until May 2. If you'd like to export your schedules before then, the export button is under Settings › Data.

The message is courteous, explains its reasoning and leaves no doubt about the answer.

## When to be direct

The indirect plan is not always best. Use a **direct approach**, putting the bad news first, when:

- the reader prefers directness, as many managers do for internal updates;
- the news is minor and the reader will not be upset;
- the reader may overlook the news if it is buried, such as a safety recall or a change to a contract;
- the situation is urgent;
- previous messages have been ignored and firmness is needed.

Internal bad news, such as a project delay, is often best reported directly with a clear explanation and a plan: "The launch will move from June 3 to June 17 because the payment provider's certification is delayed. Here is what we're doing to keep the new date."

## Apologies

When the organization has made a mistake, a sincere apology is appropriate. An effective apology acknowledges the specific error, takes responsibility, explains what is being done to fix it and to prevent a repeat, and does so promptly. Vague or conditional apologies, such as "We're sorry if anyone was inconvenienced," tend to make matters worse because they sound like they deny responsibility. Organizations sometimes worry that apologizing creates legal risk; when in doubt about the wording of a serious apology, writers consult their legal team, but they should not let that review delay the response unnecessarily.

## Bad news in person

Some bad news must be delivered in conversation, such as layoffs or performance problems. The same principles apply: prepare, state the news clearly early in the conversation, explain the reasons, listen, and discuss next steps. Delivering serious personal news by email or text is widely regarded as disrespectful.
