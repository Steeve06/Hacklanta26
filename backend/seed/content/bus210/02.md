## A process, not a single draft

Good business documents rarely arrive in one pass. A reliable approach divides the work into three stages: **planning**, **writing** and **completing**. Experienced writers often spend as much time planning and revising as they do drafting, because the thinking that happens before and after the draft is what makes a message clear.

## Planning

Planning begins with **purpose**. State in one sentence what you want the reader to know or do. If you cannot, you are not ready to write. "I want the operations team to approve two extra hours of weekend staffing by Friday" is a purpose; "I want to talk about staffing" is not.

Next comes the **audience analysis** introduced in the previous chapter, and then **gathering information**. Collect the facts, figures and examples the reader will need to act. Finally, choose a **channel** and an **organizational plan**.

## Direct and indirect organization

The most important organizational decision is where to put the main point.

The **direct approach** states the main idea first, then gives supporting details. It suits routine requests, good news and any message where the reader is likely to be pleased or neutral. It respects the reader's time and is the default for most workplace messages.

The **indirect approach** opens with context or reasons and builds toward the main idea. It suits bad news and persuasive messages to resistant readers, where leading with the conclusion might cause the reader to stop listening before understanding the reasoning. Even then, the main point should not be delayed for long; readers grow suspicious of messages that circle around their subject.

## Writing in plain language

**Plain language** means writing that the intended reader can understand the first time they read it. It is not simplistic; it is efficient. Several techniques help.

**Prefer active voice.** In active voice the subject performs the action: "The finance team approved the budget." In passive voice the subject receives the action: "The budget was approved." Passive voice is longer and can hide who is responsible. It is useful occasionally, such as when the actor is unknown or when you want to soften bad news, but active voice should be the default.

**Keep sentences short.** Aim for an average of about 15 to 20 words. Long sentences force readers to hold many ideas in working memory at once. When a sentence contains two main ideas, split it.

**Use concrete words.** Replace abstractions with specifics. "Significant cost savings" means little; "a 12 percent cut in shipping costs" means something.

**Cut empty words.** Phrases such as "at this point in time," "due to the fact that" and "in order to" can become "now," "because" and "to." Removing redundancies such as "past history" or "end result" makes writing tighter without losing meaning.

**Avoid unexplained jargon.** Technical terms are fine for readers who share them, but define them or replace them for broader audiences.

## Paragraphs and visual structure

Each paragraph should develop one idea, usually announced in a **topic sentence** at the start. Readers skim; topic sentences let them follow the argument by reading only the first line of each paragraph.

Visual structure also aids skimming. **Headings** help readers find sections in longer documents. **Bulleted lists** suit items that are parallel and can be scanned, such as steps or options. **White space** makes a page approachable. Use these tools when they reflect real structure in the content, not as decoration.

## Completing the message

Completing involves **revising**, **proofreading** and **evaluating**. Revision looks at the big picture first: is the purpose clear, is the organization right, is anything missing or unnecessary? Only then should you polish sentences and fix word choice.

Proofreading catches errors in spelling, grammar, names, numbers and dates. Mistakes in these details are disproportionately damaging because they suggest carelessness. Reading the text aloud, or reading it in a different format such as a printout, helps you notice what you skimmed over while writing.

Finally, evaluate the message from the reader's side. Would someone with no context understand what you want and by when? If the honest answer is no, the message needs another pass.

## Tone

Tone is the attitude a message conveys. Business writing should generally sound courteous, confident and conversational rather than stiff. Using **"you" attitude**, framing information around the reader's interests, makes messages more persuasive: "You'll receive your refund within five days" lands better than "We have processed your refund request." Positive wording helps too. "We can ship your order on Monday" is more welcoming than "We cannot ship your order until Monday," even though both state the same fact.
