## Speaking to be understood

Presentations remain one of the most visible forms of workplace communication. A product team presents a roadmap, a consultant presents findings to a client, a student presents a project to a panel. Unlike a document, a presentation is experienced once, at the speaker's pace. Listeners cannot reread a confusing sentence, so clarity and structure matter even more than in writing.

## Planning around one message

Start by defining your **key message**: the one idea the audience should remember if they forget everything else. Write it as a full sentence. "Moving support to a shared inbox will cut response times in half without new hires" is a key message; "Support improvements" is a topic.

Then analyze the **audience**. How much do they know? What do they care about? What decision, if any, will they make? How much time do they actually have? A presentation to executives might need the conclusion in the first minute and details only on request. A training session for new hires might need more explanation and examples.

Consider the **setting** too: the room, whether attendees are remote, the equipment, and whether you will be presenting before or after lunch. Practical details can derail a well-prepared talk.

## Structure

A clear structure helps listeners follow along.

**Opening.** Capture attention, establish why the topic matters to this audience, and preview what you will cover. Effective openings include a relevant question, a short story, a striking fact or a statement of the problem. Avoid starting with apologies or a long description of yourself.

**Body.** Organize the main points, typically no more than three or four, using a pattern that suits the content: problem and solution, chronological, by topic, or by comparison. Use clear **transitions** between points, such as "We've seen the cost of the current process; now let's look at the alternative," so listeners know where they are.

**Closing.** Summarize the key message and, where relevant, state the action you want. End deliberately rather than trailing off with "So, yeah, that's it." Many speakers plan their last sentence word for word.

## Slides that help

Slides should support the speaker, not replace them. A common failure is the slide covered in full paragraphs, which the speaker then reads aloud. The audience reads faster than the speaker talks, finishes early and stops listening.

Better slides follow a few principles.

- **One idea per slide.** A headline that states the slide's point, such as "Response times doubled after the team shrank," followed by evidence.
- **Minimal text.** Use short phrases rather than sentences. Put the detail in your speech or in a handout.
- **Strong visuals.** A well-chosen chart, diagram or photo is often more memorable than bullet points. Each chart should make one point clearly.
- **Readable design.** Large fonts, high contrast between text and background, and consistent layout. Test slides from the back of the room or on a small laptop screen.
- **Accessibility.** Do not rely on colour alone to convey meaning, and describe key visuals aloud for anyone who cannot see them.

A useful test is to cover the slide and ask whether your spoken explanation still makes sense. If it does not, you are relying on the slide too much.

## Delivery

Delivery covers voice, body language and timing. Speak at a moderate pace and vary your tone; a monotone makes even interesting content hard to follow. Pause before and after important points. Make eye contact across the room, or look into the camera when presenting online. Stand or sit upright, and use natural gestures.

**Practice** is the single best way to improve delivery. Rehearse aloud, ideally with a timer and an audience, rather than rereading notes silently. Practise the opening and the transitions most, because that is where speakers most often stumble. Many experienced presenters use brief notes or speaker notes rather than a full script, which helps them sound natural.

## Managing nerves

Speaking anxiety is extremely common, including among experienced presenters. Some arousal is helpful, as it sharpens focus. Techniques that help include thorough preparation, slow breathing before starting, reframing nervousness as excitement, and focusing on the audience's needs rather than on yourself. Audiences generally want speakers to succeed and notice nervousness far less than speakers fear.

## Handling questions

Plan for questions by anticipating the likely ones and preparing short answers or backup slides. When a question comes, listen fully, repeat or rephrase it so everyone hears it, and answer concisely. If you do not know the answer, say so and offer to follow up, then do. If a question is hostile, stay calm, acknowledge the concern, and respond to its substance rather than its tone. Bringing the session back to your key message at the end ensures it is the last thing the audience hears.
