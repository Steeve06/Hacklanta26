## Documents that inform decisions

Reports and proposals are longer, more structured documents that help organizations make decisions. They may run from a two-page memo to a hundred-page study, but they share a goal: giving decision-makers the information and analysis they need, in a form they can use quickly.

## Types of reports

**Informational reports** present facts without analysis or recommendations. Examples include monthly sales summaries, meeting minutes, incident reports and progress reports on a project. Their main requirements are accuracy, completeness and clear organization.

**Analytical reports** present facts and then interpret them, often recommending a course of action. A feasibility study asking whether to open a second location, or a report comparing three software vendors, is analytical.

**Proposals** are persuasive documents that offer to solve a problem or meet a need. They may be **internal**, such as a request to fund a new training program, or **external**, such as a bid to supply services to a client. They may be **solicited**, written in response to a request for proposals, or **unsolicited**, initiated by the writer.

## Structure of a formal report

Longer reports follow a predictable structure so that readers can find what they need. A typical analytical report contains:

- **Title page** and, for long reports, a **table of contents**.
- **Executive summary**: a concise overview of the purpose, key findings and recommendations, usually one page or less. Many readers, especially senior managers, read only this section, so it must stand on its own.
- **Introduction**: the background, purpose and scope of the report, and the methods used to gather information.
- **Findings** or **discussion**: the evidence, organized logically, often with headings for each topic or criterion.
- **Conclusions**: what the findings mean.
- **Recommendations**: specific actions the reader should take, ideally with responsibilities and timelines.
- **References** and **appendices** for supporting material such as survey instruments or detailed data.

Reports can be organized **directly**, with conclusions and recommendations immediately after the introduction, or **indirectly**, with findings first. Direct order suits readers who trust the writer and want answers fast. Indirect order suits readers who may be sceptical and need to follow the reasoning.

## Organizing findings

Findings should be organized according to what helps the reader decide. Common patterns include:

- **By criteria**: comparing options on cost, reliability, ease of use and so on.
- **By component**: discussing each department, product or region separately.
- **By time**: describing what happened in sequence, as in a progress report.
- **By importance**: starting with the most significant findings.

Whatever the pattern, use **descriptive headings** that tell the reader what each section contains. "Vendor B offers the lowest total cost" is more useful than "Cost analysis."

## Proposal essentials

A proposal must answer the reader's core questions: What problem are you solving? How will you solve it? Why are you qualified? What will it cost, and when will it be done? A typical structure includes an introduction summarizing the problem and the proposed solution, a background section showing understanding of the problem, a detailed plan, a schedule, qualifications of the team, a budget and a request for approval. For solicited proposals, follow the requester's format exactly; reviewers often score proposals against a checklist, and missing sections cost points.

## Presenting data

Data often makes or breaks a report. Choose the display that fits the message.

- **Tables** present exact values and let readers look up specific numbers.
- **Bar charts** compare quantities across categories.
- **Line charts** show change over time.
- **Pie charts** show parts of a whole, but only work well with a few categories; bar charts are usually clearer.

Every chart needs a clear title, labelled axes with units and a source. Refer to each table or chart in the text and explain what it shows. The reader should not have to work out the point alone.

Data displays can also mislead, intentionally or not. Starting a bar chart's vertical axis above zero can make small differences look dramatic. Using unequal time intervals on a line chart distorts trends. Choosing only the years that support a conclusion hides the full picture. Honest presentation shows the data as it is, notes limitations such as small samples, and lets readers draw fair conclusions.

## Citing sources

Reports must document where their information comes from. Citing sources lets readers verify claims, gives credit to others' work and protects the writer from accusations of plagiarism. Follow the citation style your organization or instructor requires, and cite paraphrased ideas as well as direct quotations.
