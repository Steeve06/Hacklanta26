## Persuasion at work

Much workplace communication is persuasive, even when it does not look like a sales pitch. Asking a manager for budget, proposing a new process, recruiting volunteers for a committee and pitching an idea to a client all involve convincing someone to think or act differently. Persuasion in this sense is not manipulation. It is the honest work of showing someone why a course of action makes sense for them.

## Three appeals

More than two thousand years ago, Aristotle described three ways to persuade, and they remain a useful framework.

**Ethos** is persuasion through the credibility of the speaker. Readers are more likely to accept a proposal from someone they see as knowledgeable, trustworthy and fair. Writers build ethos by citing relevant experience, using reliable sources, acknowledging limitations honestly and writing carefully. A single factual error can damage ethos for the entire message.

**Pathos** is persuasion through emotion and values. Facts alone rarely move people; they also need to care. A short, specific story, such as one customer's experience, can make a problem feel real in a way that statistics cannot. Pathos must be used responsibly. Exaggerating fears or inventing urgency may work once but destroys trust.

**Logos** is persuasion through logic and evidence: data, examples, comparisons and clear reasoning. In business, logos often means showing costs and benefits. "The new scheduling tool costs $4,000 a year and would save each of our eight coordinators about three hours a week" gives the reader something concrete to evaluate.

The strongest messages combine all three, weighted to suit the audience. Analytical readers respond most to logos; readers deciding whether to support a community initiative may respond more to pathos.

## The AIDA pattern

Persuasive messages, particularly to readers who may be uninterested or resistant, often follow the **AIDA** pattern.

**Attention.** Open with something that makes the reader want to keep reading: a relevant problem, a surprising fact, a question or a benefit. "Last quarter, 18 percent of our support tickets were customers asking where their order was."

**Interest.** Develop the problem or opportunity in more detail, showing why it matters to the reader specifically.

**Desire.** Present your proposal and its benefits. Provide evidence that it works: data, testimonials, results from a pilot. This is also where you address objections.

**Action.** State exactly what you want the reader to do, make it easy, and give a reason to act soon. "If you approve the pilot by May 10, we can have tracking emails running before the summer peak."

For routine requests to receptive readers, a direct approach is better: ask first, then explain. AIDA is for situations where the reader needs to be convinced before they are ready to hear the request.

## Handling objections

Resistant readers usually have reasons: cost, time, risk, or doubt that the change will work. Ignoring these makes a proposal look naive. Addressing them builds credibility.

The most effective approach is to anticipate the main objections during planning and answer them within the message, framed positively. Instead of writing "Some may think this is too expensive," write "At $4,000 a year, the tool pays for itself within four months through reduced overtime." Address the most important objections and leave minor ones for discussion; listing every possible concern can create doubts the reader did not have.

## Requests for favours

Many persuasive messages ask for help without offering anything in return, such as asking an expert to speak to your class or a colleague to review a report. These succeed when they are specific, respectful of the reader's time and clear about what is involved. State the request early, explain why you chose this person, describe exactly what is needed and by when, and make it easy to say yes, or no. Expressing appreciation regardless of the answer preserves the relationship for the future.

## Ethics of persuasion

Persuasion becomes unethical when it relies on deception, hides important information, or pressures people into decisions against their interests. Useful tests include asking whether you would be comfortable if your methods were public, and whether the reader would still agree if they had all the information you have. Ethical persuaders win agreement that lasts because it is based on understanding.
